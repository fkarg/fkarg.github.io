---
title: My Contribution to Building Software
date: 2026-11-25
tags: [ai, software]
---

AI-Assisted coding has come a long way the last 12 months. We went from 'barely
trustable, with high oversight it's _sometimes_ a speedup' to very clear
speedups across the board.

Still, AI is far from a [perfect
genie](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/4ARaTpNX62uaL86j6/the-hidden-complexity-of-wishes)
and so a number of problems remain for writing software, most of which are
about choosing directions, architecture, and the right set of tradeoffs.

Generally, even if AI is writing 99% of my code now, my main contributions fall in two categories:
- 'Don't do that' - I often have to push back in certain directions, architecturally or combinations of tradeoffs that seem _okay_ or _normal_ for most cases, but don't _quite_ fit in our constraint space. Some AI ideas need more pushback and reasoning than others. I'm giving explanations, because often this will result in most follow-up decisions _in their session_ to be of better tradeoff quality, saving me time overall.
- 'consider/research direction X' - AIs of various types are excellent for boilerplate, but good bigger-picture architectural design decisions are still harder to tease out - initial suggestions need pushback with the tradeoffs made, but often a rejection of the supposed premise is necessary to have a chance at archieving better/okay service synergy across the board - plus a hint towards exploring alternative ideas. Parts of this can be prompted/automated for, but ultimately human attention of capable individuals is the bottleneck to achieve better results across the board.

Ultimately, AI is a tool that can (and probably will) be a multiplier to your
own abilities. That also means, that it is _limited_ in multiplying your own
abilities (or, how you use it), which is also what we are seeing now across the
board. A single 'build me the next google, make no mistakes, simplify' is just not enough.

Don't forget, even if using tools to supercharge development, the IBM quote from 1979 continues to hold true:

> A computer can never be held accountable, therefore a computer must never make a management decision.
